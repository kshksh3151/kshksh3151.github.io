## Structure, Electronic Structure, and Charge Transport

Conjugated polymers occupy a unique position among electronic materials because their electrical properties are governed not only by chemical composition, but also by the way polymer chains organize, fluctuate, and interact in the solid state. Unlike conventional crystalline semiconductors, conjugated polymers are structurally heterogeneous across multiple length scales. Local backbone conformation, intermolecular packing, degree of order, and energetic disorder can all influence the electronic states available for charge transport. As a result, even polymers with similar chemical structures can exhibit markedly different electrical behaviour depending on their morphology.

This strong coupling between structure and electronic properties presents a fundamental challenge. Molecular-scale information is often available from atomistic simulation or quantum-chemical calculations, whereas experimentally measured transport properties emerge from collective behaviour over much larger spatial and temporal scales. Establishing a physically meaningful connection between these levels is therefore essential for understanding why a particular morphology leads to a certain electronic structure and, in turn, how that electronic structure governs charge localization, hopping, and macroscopic transport.

Another important complication is that structural disorder does not necessarily play a single, universally detrimental role. Disorder can alter electronic energy distributions, modify intermolecular electronic coupling, and change the spatial pathways available to charge carriers. Depending on the physical process of interest, these effects may compete with one another. A structural feature that favours one aspect of charge generation or localization may simultaneously hinder long-range transport. For this reason, simple structure–property correlations are often insufficient, and a more mechanistic description is required to identify which structural factors control which electronic processes.

My research aims to build such connections using molecular simulation and multiscale modelling. I am particularly interested in developing computational descriptions that link realistic polymer morphologies to electronic structure and charge-transport behaviour, and in identifying the physical descriptors that can explain experimentally observed trends. Ultimately, I hope to contribute to predictive frameworks in which molecular and morphological information can be used not only to interpret measurements, but also to guide the design of conjugated polymer materials.


## Electrochemical Doping and Dynamic Structural Evolution

Electrochemical doping introduces an additional level of complexity because electronic charging, ion transport, solvation, and polymer structural reorganization occur simultaneously. In mixed ionic–electronic conducting polymers, applying an electrochemical potential does not simply add or remove electronic charge. Counterions migrate into or out of the polymer phase, local electrostatic environments change, solvent molecules redistribute, and the polymer matrix can swell or reorganize in response. These processes are strongly coupled, meaning that the state of the material continuously evolves as doping proceeds.

This dynamic nature makes electrochemical doping difficult to describe using static structural models alone. The accessibility of a polymer region to an ion may depend on local packing and solvation, while the presence of newly introduced charge can in turn alter local interactions and morphology. Different ionic species can experience different energetic and steric environments, leading to ion-specific behaviour even under otherwise similar electrochemical conditions. At the same time, the resulting structural changes may affect subsequent ion transport and charge accommodation, creating feedback between doping and morphology.

Understanding these coupled processes is important because the macroscopic response of electrochemically doped polymers emerges from molecular events that are difficult to isolate experimentally. Quantities such as swelling, charging efficiency, conductivity, and response time reflect the combined influence of ion partitioning, local interactions, polymer chemistry, and structural dynamics. A molecular-level description is therefore needed to clarify which microscopic processes dominate under different conditions and why certain polymer–electrolyte combinations behave differently from others.

My current research focuses on developing simulation strategies that can describe electrochemical doping as an evolving molecular process rather than as a sequence of independent static states. I am interested in how ion identity, polymer chemistry, local solvation, and structural environment influence charging and the accompanying morphological response. In the longer term, I aim to use these insights to establish more general relationships between microscopic doping mechanisms and experimentally observable material behaviour, with the goal of informing the design of conjugated polymers for mixed ionic–electronic transport.
